Building an online course takes weeks: an outline, slides, recordings, edits, a sales page. Many creators do all of that work first and only then find out whether anyone wants to buy it. When the answer is no, the time is gone.

Preselling flips the order. You sell the course first, to a small group, with a clear promise and a clear refund. Their payment is the answer you need. This playbook walks you through a two-week presale you can run alone, step by step.

What does it mean to presell an online course?

Preselling an online course means taking real orders for a course before you create it, with an honest start date and a refund if it does not happen. The buyers are not donors. They are early students who get a lower price and a voice in the course in return for going first.

The key word is real. A "would you buy this?" survey or a free waitlist measures interest, and interest is cheap. People say yes to be kind. A payment is different: someone looked at the offer, thought about their own problem, and decided the result was worth money. That is the signal you are looking for.

Why should you presell before you build?

You should presell because it is the cheapest way to learn whether people will pay for this exact result, from you, at this price. Everything else you could measure, such as likes, followers or survey answers, sits one step away from that question.

Preselling also makes the course better. When ten people have paid, you can ask them what they are stuck on and teach exactly that. Your first lessons are shaped by real questions instead of your guess about what beginners need. A course outline written with paying students in the room is often shorter than the one you planned alone, because you only teach what they actually need.

Finally, it gives you a deadline. A course with paying students and a start date gets finished. A course without either tends to stay "almost ready" for months.

What goes into a presale offer?

A presale offer needs six things, and all of them fit on one short page or in one message. If you cannot fill a line, the course idea is not sharp enough yet, and that is useful to learn too.

PartWhat to writeExample
ResultWhat the student can do at the end"Publish your first video each week without a team"
Who it is forOne kind of person, with one problem"Solo founders who have never filmed themselves"
FormatHow it is taught and how long it takes"Four live sessions, one per week, recorded"
Start dateA real date you will keep"First session in three weeks"
Founding priceLower than the final price, and why"A thank-you for helping shape it"
PromiseWhat happens if it does not run"Full refund, no questions"

Write the result line first and spend the most time on it. It works like a landing page headline: the right reader should see their own problem in it and want the outcome. If you are unsure who the reader is, start from your ideal customer profile.

How do you run a two-week presale?

Run it as a short, fixed window: one week to talk to people, one week to open the offer and close it on a set date. The deadline is part of the method. It gives buyers a reason to decide and gives you a clear moment to read the result.

A few habits make the window work better:

  • Talk before you pitch. The calls in week one are research, not sales. Listen for the exact words people use for their problem, then use those words in the offer.
  • Invite people one by one. A personal message to someone who asked about the topic is far stronger than a public post. Share it publicly too, but second.
  • Teach while you sell. Post one free lesson on the topic during the presale. It shows how you teach and gives readers a small result first, the core idea behind education content marketing.
  • Keep a simple list. Who you asked, who answered, who bought, and why the others said no. That list is your best course outline.

How many buyers prove the idea?

Set your buyer target before you open the presale, and pick the smallest number that would make teaching the course worth your time. Writing it down first stops you from moving the goal after you see the result. For a first live cohort, a small number is fine; five or ten students is plenty to teach and learn from.

It helps to see the presale as a funnel. Each stage loses people, and that is normal. What matters is where the biggest drop happens, because that tells you what to fix.

Read the result like this. If you hit your target, build the course and start on the date you promised. If you got close, keep the offer but fix the weakest stage, such as a vaguer result or the wrong audience. If almost nobody bought, refund everyone, thank them, and ask the people who said no what would have made it a yes.

In an example like this one, price is not the main problem, so a discount would not help. Most people who said no were waiting for a better moment, which suggests a second cohort with a later start date. The next biggest group was unsure of the result, which points to a sharper result line. Each answer turns into a change you can make before the next round.

Summary

Preselling an online course means selling a clear, honest offer before you build it, then letting real payments decide what happens next. Write six lines: result, who it is for, format, start date, founding price and refund promise. Talk to people for a week, sell for a week, and close on the date you set. Hit your target and build with your first students; miss it and you have learned cheaply.

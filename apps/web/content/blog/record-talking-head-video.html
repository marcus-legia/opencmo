A talking-head video is the simplest video you can make: you, a camera, and one idea. It is also one of the most useful for a founder or creator. People buy from people they recognise, and a face and a voice build that recognition faster than text.

Most first attempts look worse than they need to, and the cause is rarely the camera. It is a dark face, an echoey room or a phone pointing up at the chin. This guide walks you through the setup in the order that matters, so your next recording looks and sounds clear with what you already own.

What do you need to record a talking-head video?

You need a phone, a window, a way to hold the phone steady at eye level, and ideally a small microphone. That is the whole kit. Everything else is an upgrade you can add later, once you know you will keep making videos.

Think of your setup as a ladder. Start on the bottom rung and climb only when a rung is solid. A founder who records weekly with a phone and a window learns more than one who waits a month for the perfect gear.

How do you light yourself at home?

Light yourself by facing a window, so daylight falls evenly on your face. Soft light from the front is the most flattering light there is, and it costs nothing. The most common mistake is the opposite: sitting with a window behind you, which turns your face into a dark shape against a bright background.

Try this test: sit where you plan to record and look at your phone's front camera. If you can see your eyes clearly and there are no deep shadows under them, the light is good. If the image is dark or flat, move closer to the window or turn so it is slightly to one side of you. When daylight isn't an option, a single soft lamp in front of you and a little above eye level does the same job.

How do you get clear sound?

Get clear sound by putting a microphone close to your mouth and recording in a room with soft surfaces. Viewers forgive a slightly soft picture, but they leave quickly when the voice is hard to hear. That makes sound the best place to spend your first bit of money.

Distance is the big factor. A phone's built-in microphone across the room picks up the echo of the room as much as your voice. A small clip-on microphone near your collar picks up mostly you. The room helps too: curtains, a sofa, a rug and a full bookshelf soak up echo, while bare walls and hard floors bounce it around.

The pattern in this example is worth remembering: the changes at the top are free or cheap, and the expensive one sits at the bottom. Before you buy anything, record ten seconds, listen back with headphones, and fix what you hear first.

How should you frame the shot?

Frame the shot with the lens at your eye level, about an arm's length away, with your eyes roughly a third of the way down the screen. A lens below your eyes looks up your nose; a lens far above makes you look small. Eye level feels like a conversation, which is exactly the feeling you want.

  • Pick the shape first. Record vertical for short-form feeds and horizontal for longer lessons. Cropping later always loses part of the picture.
  • Leave a little space above your head. Not much: a sliver of room keeps you from looking cramped.
  • Step away from the wall. A little distance between you and the background adds depth and softens shadows.
  • Keep the background simple. Remove clutter and leave one or two objects that say something about you.
  • Look at the lens, not the screen. The lens is your viewer's eyes. A small sticker next to it is a useful reminder.

How do you record without freezing up?

Record without freezing by working from short bullet points and recording one point at a time. Trying to deliver a whole video in one perfect take is the fastest way to get stuck. Short sections are easier to say well, and the cuts between them are normal on screen.

A few habits make the take easier. Start each section with a short breath and a small smile; it lifts your voice. Speak a little slower than feels natural, since nerves speed everyone up. When you make a mistake, don't stop the recording: pause, and start the sentence again. The pause makes the cut easy to find later.

Once you have a recording, one long video can feed a whole week of posts. Our playbook on short-form video for founders shows how to turn it into a routine. The editing itself can be quick: an editor such as OpenCMO lets you upload your recording and cut it by its words, then add captions.

Summary

A clear talking-head video comes from a few simple choices, not expensive gear. Face a window for soft light, put a microphone close to your mouth in a soft room, and place the lens at eye level about an arm's length away. Record short sections from bullet points, keep the bottom of the frame free for captions, and cut the pauses afterwards. Then record again next week: each video teaches you what to fix in the next one.
